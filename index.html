Problem Summary

Flowers have intricate three dimensional (3D) structures that play vital roles in ecological and evolutionary processes such as pollination, species differentiation, and reproductive adaptation. However, most morphological studies still rely on 2D representations, limiting the accuracy of structural analysis. Traditional 3D reconstruction methods like photogrammetry and micro-computed tomography (micro CT) offer partial solutions but come with significant drawbacks such as high cost, complex setup, limited color fidelity, and incompatibility with in-field imaging.

Given the rapid degradation of flowers after collection due to wilting, loss of color, and structural collapse capturing their morphology quickly and accurately is essential. This project explores state-of-the-art neural 3D reconstruction methods using accessible smartphone imaging in the wild or field conditions, enabling scalable, structure and color-preserving digitization before delicate traits are lost.

Previous Work

Traditional techniques for 3D flower modeling primarily include micro-computed tomography(micro CT) and photogrammetry: Micro CT scanning, used in studies such as Dellinger et al. (2019), provides high-resolution reconstructions of internal and external floral structures. However, it does not preserve color, involves destructive sampling, and requires specialized equipment, making it inaccessible for in field digitization. ABoth methods are limited by high cost, infrastructure needs, and lack of portability, making them impractical for large scale or on-site botanical documentation, particularly for fragile floral structures that degrade quickly.

Photogrammetry, as demonstrated by Leménager et al. (2023), uses structured image capture (DSLR camera, turntable, controlled lighting) to generate 3D models. While it captures both shape and color, it is time-consuming, expensive, and unsuitable for field deployment due to its reliance on controlled environments and extensive manual processing.

Related Work

Recent advances in neural 3D reconstruction offer promising alternatives to traditional approaches: Neuralangelo (Li et al., 2023) is a learning-based neural surface reconstruction method that can generate high fidelity 3D geometry from posed images. It achieves fine detail but is computationally intensive and primarily evaluated under lab-like, static settings. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) and 2DGS (Huang et al., 2024) are efficient neural techniques for geometry aware radiance field rendering. These methods allow for fast, high-quality reconstructions with significantly reduced training time compared to NeRF style models. While these methods have demonstrated strong performance in structured, well-lit scenes with clean inputs, their application to micro-structured biological targets like flowers, especially under field conditions with smartphone cameras has not been explored. This project bridges that gap by evaluating how well these neural methods perform in realistic, low-resource settings and by identifying adaptations required for modeling delicate floral traits in 3D.

Analysis of Work

New Results

This study is one of the first to apply neural reconstruction methods to micro-structured biological samples such as flowers using field captured smartphone imagery. It demonstrates the feasibility of using 3DGS for detailed flower modeling without expensive lab setups.

Meeting Goals

Initial goals were partially met: 3DGS was successfully tested, and Neuralangelo training has begun. Delays in hardware availability and long training times affected the ability to complete Neuralangelo and 2DGS experiments during the initial phase.

Future Work

Short-Term Goals

  • Complete Neuralangelo and 2DGS training and evaluation.
  • Conduct side-by-side comparison of all models using shape fidelity, texture detail, and performance metrics.
  • Validate models using biological benchmarks (e.g., morphometric accuracy).

Long-Term Goals

  • Develop a field-ready protocol using smartphones and macro lenses.
  • Publish a 3D flower dataset with taxonomic metadata for research use.
  • Explore fine-tuning neural models specifically for botanical datasets.
  • Contribute to digital herbarium platforms and conservation tools through scalable 3D digitization.

Open Questions

  • Scale Adaptability: How can neural reconstruction methods be adapted to handle the extreme scale and detail required for tiny flowers (e.g., grass inflorescences)?
  • Lighting & Focus Challenges: Can these models tolerate uneven lighting, depth-of-field issues, or motion blur common in field-based smartphone captures?
  • Training Data Requirements: What kind of training data or augmentations improve performance for micro-structured biological samples?
  • Field Readiness: What is the minimal imaging setup (e.g., phone + macro lens) that still yields usable 3D reconstructions in natural conditions?
  • Biological Validation: How well do the reconstructed models preserve biologically relevant traits for morphometric, ecological, or taxonomic studies?
